Wild Rift Challenger: Requirements and a Climb Plan

By Danny · Ranked improvement · Updated 21 September 2026

Challenger is the standard Wild Rift ranked tier above Grandmaster and below Sovereign. To work toward it, separate two questions: what the current ranked system requires, and which decisions you need to improve in your own games.

What rank is Challenger in Wild Rift?

In standard Ranked, the upper tiers run from Master to Grandmaster, Challenger and Sovereign. Legendary Ranked is a separate ladder. Riot explains the distinction in its Ranked overview.

Riot's Ranked Marks reference lists 60 Marks for Challenger and 100 for Sovereign. That reference is dated November 2023 and some of its other details are older than the current Ranked overview. Check your in-game Ranked panel for the requirement in your current season and region before planning around a number.

Marks measure ladder progress; they are not a promise that a fixed number of games will get you there. Wins, losses and the season's progression systems affect the climb. For the difference between visible rank and matchmaking, read MMR and matchmaking explained.

The practice plan below focuses on decisions you can review. It is a two-week training block, not a promise to reach Challenger in two weeks.

Start with one main role

One main role makes improvement much faster. Every role in Wild Rift has different responsibilities. Jungle controls tempo and objectives. Mid influences side lanes and river. ADC needs positioning and damage discipline. Support creates vision, engage timing, and protection. Baron lane requires matchup knowledge, wave control, and side-lane pressure.

If you swap roles constantly, you split your learning. You may get better at the game generally, but you will not master the repeated situations that decide ranked games. Pick one main role and build your climb around it. It is fine to have one backup role, but the backup should also be simple and prepared. If you are unsure where to start, use the Wild Rift role coaching guide to compare the main responsibilities by role.

Keep your champion pool small

A small champion pool is one of the strongest climbing tools. I usually recommend one or two champions for your main role. One champion already has thousands of possible situations: different matchups, jungle paths, wave states, objective timings, teamfight angles, and item choices. Adding more champions multiplies the situations you need to understand.

When you focus on one or two champions, you start recognizing patterns faster. You know which trades are winning, when your power spike arrives, which fights are bad, and how your champion should move around objectives. That familiarity gives you more mental space for macro.

Learn objective tempo

Many players lose because they arrive late. They fight after the wave is bad, recall when dragon is spawning, or wander between camps without a clear next move. High elo players think ahead. Before you move, you should already know the next few actions: clear, reset, path to the strong side, prepare dragon, trade Herald, or pressure tower.

This is especially important for jungle coaching, but every role needs tempo. If you are ADC, you need to know when to push and recall before dragon. If you are support, you need to move early enough to place vision. If you are mid, you need to control the wave before roaming. Challenger players often win the fight before it starts because they arrive with better setup.

The Diamond to Master bridge

Most players do not jump from Diamond habits straight to Challenger habits. The usual bridge is cleaning the Diamond to Master phase first: fewer champion swaps, better recall timing, clearer objective trades, and less panic fighting when the game feels even. If your climb stalls there, read the Diamond to Master Wild Rift guide and compare it with the common Wild Rift macro mistakes checklist.

Your role decides which habit matters most. Junglers should focus on pathing and objective tempo, mid laners on wave control and roam timing, ADC players on safe damage windows, supports on vision and map impact, and Baron laners on side-lane wave management.

A 14-day practice plan for the climb

Keep the role and champion pool stable so you can compare similar decisions. If you miss a session, continue with the next step; there is no need to cram extra games into the next day.

  1. Days 1–2: find the repeated mistake. Review three recent games. Write down the first avoidable death, the recall before an important objective, and the decision after your team won a fight. Choose the mistake that repeats.
  2. Days 3–5: work on one lane or clear habit. For a laner, record waves lost because of an unnecessary roam or late reset. For a jungler, review detours that leave camps, a reset and an objective competing for the same time.
  3. Days 6–8: prepare the next objective. Check the wave, your gold to spend, nearby allies and enemy positions before moving. Count setups where you reached a useful position with resources, rather than counting objectives your team happened to secure.
  4. Days 9–11: convert an advantage. After a won fight, choose a reachable tower, objective, safe camp or reset. Review chases that gave the enemy time to respawn without your team gaining anything.
  5. Days 12–14: compare the same decisions. Review three more games using the original checklist. Keep practicing the habit if the error repeats; choose a new focus only when the first one is more consistent.

Use the champion pool guide, wave management guide and replay checklist when those decisions need more explanation. Judge a short block by the habit as well as its results; a small run of wins or losses can be misleading.

Example: a good score, a bad reset

Imagine a mid laner is ahead in kills but stays for another wave while an objective is about to become contestable. They recall after their support enters river. The team loses the fight before mid can return.

The useful review question is whether the extra wave was worth arriving late. Pause before that wave: could mid spend their gold, return with resources and help establish vision? If the river was already lost, could they signal a trade instead? This is an illustrative decision, not a recorded student result.

In the next session, the practice target is a prepared reset before the objective you intend to contest. The target is more useful than “get a better KDA.” The objective control guide explains how to choose between contesting, turning and trading.

Review your losses correctly

A useful VOD review does not ask, “Who inted?” It asks, “What repeated mistake can I remove?” Look at deaths, lost objectives, bad recalls, and fights where you entered late or without cooldowns. The goal is to find patterns. Maybe you always recall too late before dragon. Maybe you chase kills after winning a fight instead of taking tower. Maybe you play too many champions and never know your damage limits.

Reviewing one replay with focus is better than playing ten games on autopilot. If you do not know what to look for, a Challenger coach can help you identify the most important mistake first.

Protect your mental

A difficult game can still offer a comeback, so look for realistic win conditions before giving up. Shutdowns, Baron, Elder, and one clean teamfight can change everything. At the same time, you need discipline outside the match. If you lose three games in a row, take a break. Playing angry usually creates worse decisions and makes the climb longer.

I recommend playing consistently, ideally every day if possible, but not grinding endlessly. More than three or four hours a day often lowers focus. Challenger climbing is about quality sessions, not emotional queueing.

When coaching helps

Coaching helps when you are serious but cannot clearly see what is holding you back. A good coach gives you priorities: what to fix first, what to ignore for now, and how to practice in your next sessions. That is how you turn effort into faster ranked progress.

Wild Rift Challenger FAQ

Is Challenger the highest rank in Wild Rift?

No. In standard Ranked, Sovereign is above Challenger. Legendary Ranked has its own ladder, so check which queue a rank refers to.

How long does it take to reach Challenger?

There is no reliable fixed number of days or games. Your starting rank, results, available practice time and current season rules all matter. Use the 14-day plan to improve a repeatable habit, not as a rank guarantee.

Can you work toward Challenger in solo queue?

Yes. Prepare a small champion pool and a backup role, then practice decisions that do not require a coordinated team: safe waves, useful recalls, objective preparation and disciplined fight selection.

What should I review first if I am stuck?

Start with the first avoidable death and the setup before a lost objective in three recent games. Choose a repeated decision you could change with the information you had at the time.
